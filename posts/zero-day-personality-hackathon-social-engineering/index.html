Confessions of a cybersecurity student who finally stopped lurking in the corner of the room.

So it’s been about three months since I last wrote anything here. No excuse, really. I just went dark. Full radio silence. If this blog had uptime monitoring, it would have paged someone.

Here’s what actually happened during the outage: I was heads-down on VigilantX, still grinding on a cybersecurity project I’ve been building for about a year now, and somewhere in the middle of all that I also picked up a hardware project on the side. Classic move for someone who already had zero free time. But that’s not the part I actually want to talk about.

The real story from these three months isn’t a project. It’s a personality patch I didn’t know I needed.

The Bug in My Own Code

For most of my life, talking to a stranger felt like running unsigned code from an unknown source. High risk, unclear payload, why would I even execute that. If I didn’t already know you, I had zero interest in finding out who you were.

Somewhere in the middle of this networking arc I’ve been on, hopping between hackathons, AI summits, startup meetups, and cybersecurity meetups, something flipped. I’d see someone I’d never met, some random guy or girl standing near the snack table looking equally lost, and instead of my usual avoidance script, I’d just walk up and start talking. I still don’t fully know what triggers it. Curiosity, maybe. I’m not a neuroscientist, so don’t quote me on the brain chemistry. All I know is the exploit worked, and it worked on me.

I started treating every event like scope for recon. Not in a sleazy “collect business cards” way. More like: this room is full of people who each know one specific thing better than I do, and I have no idea which one of them holds the answer I’ll need six months from now.

Networking Is Just Distributed Debugging

Here’s the actual insight, and it’s a boring one if I’m honest, but boring insights are usually the ones that hold up.

When you hit a wall on a specific problem, in a specific domain, and you’re basically a blank hello on that topic, having one person in your circle who actually knows it is worth more than a hundred Google searches. I’ve felt this directly. I go in cold on a subject, ask a dumb opening question to someone I met at a hackathon a month back, and suddenly I have an actual answer instead of a Stack Overflow thread from 2014 with no accepted solution.

That’s the whole trick. You don’t need to know everything. You need to know one person for every gap in your knowledge, and the only way to build that roster is to talk to people before you need them.

This showed up everywhere over the past few months. At VIT Recursion Edition 2.0, our team ended up pulling in someone we’d only just met for a track we hadn’t originally planned to enter. At the AWS Student Builder Group I lead, half the people on the core team came from conversations that started as small talk at some unrelated event. Even the Dream2Reality submission for VigilantX had a team lineup that only made sense because I’d been networking hard enough to have options when a seat needed filling.

None of that happens if you’re still running the old “don’t talk to strangers” firmware.

Brute-Forcing Confidence, One Conversation at a Time

I’m not going to pretend this turned me into some smooth extrovert who works a room like a conference keynote speaker. It didn’t. I still get that pre-conversation flinch every single time. The difference is I don’t let the flinch win anymore. I just start talking, badly if needed, and let the conversation sort itself out.

Think of it like brute-forcing a login. You don’t need the perfect opener. You just need enough attempts that eventually something lands, and you walk away from an AI summit or a startup meetup with one more name in your contacts who actually remembers you.

The Actual Takeaway

If there’s one thing worth taking from three months of hackathons, meetups, and way too much free conference coffee, it’s this: your network is your real attack surface for opportunity. Not your resume. Not your GitHub. The people who’ll answer your 11pm message when you’re stuck on something is what actually moves things forward.

So go talk to that fellow standing alone by the registration desk. Worst case, nothing happens. Best case, they’re the person who saves your project six months from now.

That’s it for this post. Back to building. VigilantX, the hardware project, and whatever the next hackathon throws at me are all still very much in progress. More on those soon, hopefully in less than three months this time.
